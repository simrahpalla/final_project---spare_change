Spare Change

Turn spare parts into spare change. Spare yourself the downtime.

Simrah Palla · MGT 409 AI Foundations for Managers, Yale SOM · Final project, October 2026
Repo: https://github.com/simrahpalla/final_project---spare_change · Live app: not hosted; it runs locally from a fresh clone (README, one command)

What it does: Spare Change is a marketplace for industrial spare parts where AI agents turn a plant's messy description of a surplus part into a safe listing, find the plants whose machines actually use that part, and negotiate the price on both sides within private limits enforced in code, with a human approving every deal.

Executive summary. Plants scrap or shelve spare parts that another plant, running the same older machine, urgently needs. In 21 measured runs, the agents turned messy text into listings with 126/126 fields correct and put the right buyer first in 18/18 labelled cases. Across 42 negotiations they closed 33 deals with 0 limit violations. A typical seller run costs $0.00099; one active plant costs about $0.024 a month. Every attack, malfunction and overspending test passed (10/10, 8/8, 6/6). No independent user has tried it yet: that pilot is the next step.

2. Motivation

Who has the problem. Two kinds of plants, often running the same aging equipment. A seller plant has upgraded a line and is left with working spares (PLC modules, drives, pumps, bearings) and near-expiry chemicals that nobody in the plant will use again. A buyer plant has a line that is down because one of those exact parts failed, and the manufacturer no longer ships it.

How they cope today. The seller usually scraps the parts or leaves them on a shelf. Liquidators put scrap recovery at roughly 5–15% of original value (Maas Companies), and holding inventory is commonly estimated to cost 15–30% of its value per year (Cleverence). A 2025 industry survey found that about 22% of spare-parts inventory had not been used in five years (SPARETECH survey via Reliamag). The buyer searches eBay, calls brokers, or waits for a new part: a new replacement can take 16–20 weeks, while a matching surplus part can arrive in 2–10 days (Automa).

What it costs. Siemens estimates unplanned downtime costs the world's 500 largest companies about 11% of revenue, roughly $1.4 trillion a year; even in the lowest-cost sector, consumer goods, an hour of downtime costs about $36,000. Restarting after an incident now takes 81 minutes on average, up from 49 in 2019, partly because "emergency replacements are more challenging to source and take longer to arrive" (Siemens, True Cost of Downtime 2024).

Why now. According to industry distributors, new parts for Siemens' SIMATIC S7-300, a widely used PLC family (a PLC is the industrial computer that runs a machine), stopped shipping in October 2025, with spare parts guaranteed only until 2033 (Eichler). Plants still running S7-300s increasingly depend on spares and surplus. The money lost on both sides is real; what is missing is a way to connect the shelf in one plant to the stopped line in another.

Our four differences:

  1. Matching on what buyers run, not keywords. Code finds the plants whose installed machines (or open "wanted" posts) fit the part; the agent only ranks those, and every reason it gives is fact-checked against the database.
  2. Agents on both sides, with private limits enforced in code. A seller agent and a buyer agent negotiate; each can see only its own company's limit, and the server refuses any offer outside it.
  3. The whole loop. From a messy sentence to a compliant listing, matched buyers, alerts, negotiation and a deal both companies approve.
  4. Impact on every deal. Dollars recovered versus scrap and estimated CO2e avoided, computed in code from sourced factors.

Honest comparison. Automa and SPARETECH are closest. Automa already sells the same goods and matches surplus against parts and supplier catalogs, and SPARETECH owns the clean parts data that compatibility matching needs. Spare Change's addition is putting the matching, the two-sided negotiation and the human-approved close into one agent workflow. Its weakness against both is scale: they have real catalogs and customers; we have a synthetic demo market.

What's new versus my HW5. The architecture pattern is reused from my HW5 (PydanticAI agents on gpt-6-luna, an MCP server as the only path to the database, a FastAPI backend, an audit trail and run caps). Everything else is new: the industrial-surplus domain and dataset, all the agents, the compliance rules, two-sided matching with alerts, the agent-versus-agent negotiation with code-enforced private limits, the two human touchpoints, the impact layer and the demo accounts.

4. The agents

The organiser is code, not an agent. A Python broker runs every post through fixed steps and calls a model only where judgment is needed. Each agent returns a typed output (a fixed schema) and reaches data only through MCP tools (MCP, the Model Context Protocol, is a standard way to give an agent a fixed set of tools). The MCP server registers tools by role, so a tool outside an agent's role does not exist for it.

Model choice. All agents use gpt-6-luna through Portkey. It is the model the course key allows, and it is cheap enough ($0.10 per million input tokens) to run several agents on every post. The work is extraction, scoring and short negotiation turns, which a small model handles well when its output is a typed schema. It is a reasoning model and ignores temperature, so consistency was measured by repeat runs. Anything that doesn't need judgment (compliance rules, candidate search, limits, alerts, impact maths) is code: cheaper, and not open to persuasion.

One real run, traced step by step

On 26 October 2026, after a reset, I ran one deal end to end in the app (run ids and audit entries: output/phase11_trace.md).

Step 1: the seller posts. Hartwell Precision (Dayton, OH) pastes: "clearing out old S7-300 stuff after line 2 retrofit to 1500s. have 3x CPU 315-2DP (6ES7 315-2AG10)… ~$1425 ea." The pre-screen finds no injection; the listing agent identifies part 6ES7315-2AG10-0AB0, 3 units, used-working; the compliance rules and guard pass it (Figure 1). This run made 5 model calls and cost $0.00111.

Step 2: matches with reasons, and a price. Code finds three plants that run S7-300s; the matching agent ranks them and every reason is fact-checked. Riverbend Foods scores 98: it has an open request for this exact part and its line is down. The pricing agent suggests $1,425 (new $2,966, scrap $45.64) and a private floor, which Hartwell confirms: human touchpoint 1 (Figure 2).

Step 3: the buyer is alerted and the agents negotiate. Riverbend gets a match alert, and because both companies turned on auto-negotiate and both limits are confirmed, code starts the negotiation (Figure 3). The seller agent opens at $1,425; the buyer agent, whose line is down, accepts in round 2 (Figures 4–5). The negotiation made 6 model calls and cost $0.00079. Each side sees only its own limit: the seller's floor ($998) and the buyer's budget ($1,890) never appear in the other's view.

Step 4: both companies approve. The deal waits until each company approves its own side: human touchpoint 2. It then closes at $1,425, $465 under the buyer's budget; the listing keeps its remaining units and the request is marked filled. The Marketplace shows the closed deal's impact (Figures 6–7).

Design choices that matter. (1) The MCP server is the only path to the database, so an agent can do only what its role's tools allow. (2) Each negotiator runs in its own process bound to one negotiation and can read only its own limit. (3) Processes are started once and reused; starting one per run crashed under load (see Safety). (4) Every agent call writes its tokens and cost to a token_usage table and every tool call to an append-only audit trail.

Per user and at scale. Assumption (stated, not measured): one active plant runs 10 seller posts, 4 buyer requests and 6 negotiations a month. That costs $0.024 per user per month. At 100 users: $2.43 a month ($29.14 a year). At 10,000 users: $242.80 a month ($2,913.60 a year). For scale, the US has about 280,000 manufacturing establishments, so 10,000 users would be about 3.6% of them. Model cost is not the constraint; hosting, support and sales (not costed here) would be.

What keeps the cost down.

  • Code before models: the pre-screen and compliance rules stopped the 6 planted bad posts with 0 model calls; a request with no compatible listing skips the matching agent.
  • SQL pre-filter: the matching agent ranks only the few plants that code found compatible, not every company.
  • Prompt caching: 31% of seller-run input and 57% of negotiation input were served from cache, at a tenth of the price.
  • Caps: at most 6 negotiation rounds, 30 model calls and 150,000 tokens per run.

7. Impact

Measured in our runs (synthetic market). Of the 33 logged test deals, 31 are used for prices and impact (2 used a listing or request created during a test run, which the seed catalog can't price). On average a deal recovered $1,889 against $63 of scrap value and avoided an est. 14.0 kg CO2e (the 28 non-chemical deals). Deals closed at 42% of the new price (range 23%–64%). Test deals repeat the same pairs, so these are averages, never totals, and not real-world results.

How CO2e is estimated. est. CO2e avoided = part weight × a material factor (steel 1.95 kg CO2e/kg; cast iron 1.86), or a per-unit factor for electronics (14 kg per PLC CPU, 12.4 per module, from Siemens product footprints), × a 0.5 reuse credit. The credit is conservative: remanufacturing studies find 46–93% lower emissions. Chemicals get no CO2 claim; instead the app shows disposal cost avoided (about $242 per 55-gallon drum). Downtime is valued only for purchases that fill a request marked line down (8 of the 31 test deals were), and only in the plant model, at one hour each.

8. Limits and next steps

What doesn't work yet.

  • Synthetic data. 50 companies, 27 listings, 21 needs: real part numbers and list prices, made-up companies and asks. Accuracy is measured against my own answer keys.
  • Demo accounts, not logins. Switching companies is a stand-in for sign-in; private limits are still never shown to the other company.
  • No payments, shipping, or part verification. A deal ends at approval.
  • The carbon method is simplified: cradle-to-gate factors, estimated weights, shipping not subtracted.
  • No independent user test yet. The only timing evidence is my own timed test.
  • Known errors: the compliance guard wrongly flagged 1 of 21 real buy requests; matching was tuned on a small market.

Business challenges. Getting buyers and sellers on at the same time (a marketplace's chicken-and-egg problem); equipment data is scattered and private, and matching depends on it; trust and counterfeits (a used PLC needs its revision and firmware checked); and small transactions earn thin margins.

Next steps.

  1. Pilot with a plant maintenance manager: time listing a part and finding three buyers, today's way versus Spare Change, and ask whether the matches and suggested limits make sense.
  2. Start narrow: discontinued PLCs and drives, where downtime is costly and compatibility is well defined.
  3. First customer: an internal exchange between the sites of one large manufacturer, which shares one equipment database and avoids the trust problem.
  4. Verification: firmware and revision checks, photos, and seller ratings before deals close.

Your next spare part is someone's spare change.
